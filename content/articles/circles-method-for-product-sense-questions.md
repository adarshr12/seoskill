# The CIRCLES Method for Product Sense Questions

CIRCLES is a step-by-step structure, developed by Lewis Lin, for answering product design and [product sense interview questions](/pm-interview-prep/product-sense-interview-questions-explained) — questions like "design a product for X" or "how would you improve Y." The acronym stands for **C**omprehend the situation, **I**dentify the customer, **R**eport customer needs, **C**ut through prioritization, **L**ist solutions, **E**valuate trade-offs, and **S**ummarize your recommendation — a full sequence for organizing an otherwise open-ended, potentially rambling answer.

## Quick facts

- CIRCLES stands for: **C**omprehend, **I**dentify the customer, **R**eport needs, **C**ut through prioritization, **L**ist solutions, **E**valuate trade-offs, **S**ummarize.
- It's a more detailed, step-by-step version of the general structured approach described in [Product Sense Interview Questions Explained](/pm-interview-prep/product-sense-interview-questions-explained).
- Created by Lewis Lin, a well-known interview coach in the product management space.
- The framework works best as a mental checklist to guide genuine reasoning, not a script to recite word for word.

## The seven steps, explained

| Step | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **C**omprehend the situation | Clarify the question — what product, what goal, any ambiguity worth resolving upfront |
| **I**dentify the customer | Define specifically who you're designing for |
| **R**eport customer needs | Identify the real problems or needs this customer segment has |
| **C**ut through prioritization | Decide which need to focus on, and explain why |
| **L**ist solutions | Generate multiple possible solutions for the prioritized need |
| **E**valuate trade-offs | Weigh the pros and cons of your top solution options |
| **S**ummarize your recommendation | State your final recommendation clearly, with brief reasoning |

## A full worked example: "Design a product for busy parents"

**Comprehend:** "To clarify — are we designing a brand-new product, or a feature for an existing one? I'll assume this is a new, standalone product concept."

**Identify the customer:** "I'll focus on parents of young children, roughly ages 2-8, who are juggling full-time work alongside parenting responsibilities."

**Report needs:** "Likely needs include: coordinating schedules across school, activities, and childcare; reducing the mental load of tracking many small logistics; and finding brief pockets of quality time despite limited availability."

**Cut through prioritization:** "I'd focus on reducing the mental load of coordination, since this seems like a frequent, high-friction pain point that affects daily life directly, more so than the other needs identified."

**List solutions:** "A few options: a shared family calendar with automatic reminders, a delegation tool for splitting logistics tasks between co-parents or caregivers, or an AI assistant that proactively surfaces upcoming logistics conflicts before they become last-minute problems."

**Evaluate trade-offs:** "A shared calendar is simple to build but is already a crowded space with many existing tools. The AI assistant is more differentiated but harder to build well and requires trust in its proactive suggestions. The delegation tool addresses a specific, less-served need — coordinating between multiple caregivers — with less existing competition."

**Summarize:** "I'd recommend starting with the delegation tool, since it addresses a specific, underserved need with less direct competition, and could expand toward the AI assistant concept once we have real usage data and trust established with users."

## Why the "Cut through prioritization" step matters so much

A common weakness in product sense answers is identifying several plausible customer needs, then trying to design a solution addressing all of them simultaneously — producing an unfocused, sprawling answer. The CIRCLES method's explicit prioritization step forces a candidate to make a real, defensible choice about what matters most, then focus the rest of the answer there — mirroring the real skill product managers need on the job, where focus and prioritization matter more than generating an exhaustive list of possibilities.

## How CIRCLES compares to a general structured approach

CIRCLES is essentially a more detailed, explicitly named version of the general structured approach (clarify, identify problems, prioritize, propose solutions, measure success) covered in [Product Sense Interview Questions Explained](/pm-interview-prep/product-sense-interview-questions-explained). Some candidates find CIRCLES's explicit seven-step structure easier to remember and apply consistently; others find a looser, more general structure feels more natural and less rigid in the moment. Either approach works — what matters most is genuinely organizing your answer with clear reasoning, not which specific named framework you use.

## Common mistakes when using CIRCLES

- **Reciting the acronym steps mechanically without genuinely reasoning through each one.** Interviewers can tell when a candidate is following a memorized script rather than actually thinking through the specific question.
- **Spending too much time on Comprehend and Identify, leaving little time for the more important Cut through prioritization and Evaluate trade-offs steps.**
- **Skipping the Evaluate trade-offs step entirely**, jumping straight from listing solutions to a recommendation without showing the reasoning that led there.
- **Treating every step as equally important to spend time on**, rather than adjusting emphasis based on what the specific question actually calls for.

## FAQ

**Who created the CIRCLES method?**
Lewis Lin, an interview coach well known in the product management interview preparation space, developed and popularized this specific framework.

**Should I memorize the CIRCLES acronym before an interview?**
It can help as a mental checklist to make sure you cover the key steps, but reciting it rigidly or announcing each step by name during the actual interview often feels unnatural — better to internalize the underlying structure and let it guide a genuine, conversational answer.

**Is CIRCLES only useful for "design a product for X" questions, or does it work for "how would you improve Y" questions too?**
It works for both — the same underlying structure (clarify, identify the customer and needs, prioritize, generate and evaluate solutions, recommend) applies whether you're designing something new or improving an existing product.

**How is CIRCLES different from just using common sense to structure an answer?**
CIRCLES formalizes what strong, naturally structured answers already tend to do — it's most useful for candidates who find open-ended interview questions genuinely difficult to organize on the spot, giving them an explicit checklist to fall back on under interview pressure.
