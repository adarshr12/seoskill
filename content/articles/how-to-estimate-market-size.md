# How to Estimate Market Size (TAM, SAM, SOM Explained)

**TAM (Total Addressable Market)** is the total demand for a product or service if you captured 100% of the market. **SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market)** is the portion of that market you could realistically serve given your specific product and business model. **SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market)** is what you could realistically capture given competition and current resources. Together, these three figures move from a theoretical maximum down to a realistic near-term target.

## Quick facts

- TAM > SAM > SOM — each layer narrows the market to a more realistic, specific figure.
- These figures are used both for business planning and as a common PM interview estimation exercise.
- This connects to [Guesstimate Framework Step by Step](/pm-interview-prep/guesstimates/guesstimate-framework-step-by-step), the general reasoning approach used to calculate each figure.

## TAM, SAM, and SOM compared

| | Definition | Example (for a project management SaaS tool) |
|---|---|---|
| TAM | Total market demand if you captured 100% of it | All companies globally that could use project management software |
| SAM | The portion of TAM your specific product/model could serve | Companies in your target industries and geographies, within your pricing model |
| SOM | What you could realistically capture given competition and resources | Your realistic customer base in your first few years of focused go-to-market effort |

## How to calculate each figure

**TAM** is typically calculated top-down (starting from a broad industry or population figure and narrowing) or bottom-up (starting from a unit of value — like a per-customer price — and multiplying by the total potential customer count). A bottom-up approach is often considered more defensible, since it's grounded in your specific pricing and unit economics rather than a broad industry report figure that may not map cleanly to your specific product.

**SAM** narrows TAM to the portion actually reachable given your specific product's constraints — geography you actually operate in, industries your product genuinely fits, and any regulatory or technical limitations that exclude part of the broader TAM.

**SOM** narrows SAM further to a realistic near-term capture target, factoring in competition, your current resources, sales/marketing capacity, and how quickly you could realistically acquire customers given these constraints.

## A worked example

**Product:** A project management tool for small marketing agencies.

**TAM (bottom-up):** Assume there are roughly 500,000 marketing agencies globally, and your average customer would pay $2,000/year. TAM = 500,000 × $2,000 = $1 billion.

**SAM:** Narrowing to agencies in your currently supported languages and regions (say, English-speaking markets, roughly 30% of the global total) and excluding very large agencies your product isn't designed for (removing another 10%): SAM ≈ $1 billion × 30% × 90% ≈ $270 million.

**SOM:** Given current competition and a realistic 3-year go-to-market capacity, you might reasonably target capturing 2% of SAM: SOM ≈ $270 million × 2% ≈ $5.4 million in annual revenue as a realistic near-term target.

## Why this framework matters

TAM, SAM, and SOM together tell a more complete and credible story than any single figure alone — TAM shows the theoretical scale of the opportunity, but citing TAM alone (a common mistake) overstates realistic near-term potential. SAM and SOM ground the opportunity in genuine constraints, showing not just how big the market theoretically is, but how much of it your specific business could realistically capture given real-world limitations.

## Common mistakes when estimating market size

- **Citing only TAM without SAM or SOM**, overstating the realistic size of the opportunity and undermining credibility with investors or stakeholders who recognize this gap.
- **Using an inflated, poorly sourced TAM figure** from a broad industry report that doesn't map cleanly to your specific product.
- **Skipping the bottom-up calculation entirely**, relying only on top-down industry figures that can be harder to defend under scrutiny.
- **Not clearly explaining the assumptions behind SAM and SOM narrowing**, leaving the final figures unconvincing without visible reasoning.

## FAQ

**Should TAM be calculated top-down or bottom-up?**
A bottom-up approach (starting from your specific pricing and realistic customer count) is often considered more defensible and credible than a top-down approach relying on broad industry reports, though both can be used together to cross-check each other.

**How is TAM/SAM/SOM used in a PM interview context?**
It's a common market-sizing estimation exercise, testing the same structured breakdown and reasoning skills as other guesstimate questions, applied specifically to market opportunity sizing.

**What's a reasonable SOM target for a new product?**
This varies enormously by industry and competitive landscape, but SOM is typically a small single-digit percentage of SAM for a realistic near-term (1-3 year) target, reflecting genuine resource and competitive constraints.

**Do investors expect precise TAM/SAM/SOM figures?**
No — reasonable, well-reasoned estimates with clearly stated assumptions are expected; investors are generally more interested in the logic behind the numbers than false precision in the figures themselves.
