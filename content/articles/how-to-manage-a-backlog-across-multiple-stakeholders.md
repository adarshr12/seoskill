# How to Manage a Backlog Across Multiple Stakeholders

**TL;DR**
- Use one consistent prioritization framework applied transparently to every stakeholder's requests, not case-by-case judgment calls.
- Make the backlog and its reasoning visible to all stakeholders, so priority decisions don't feel arbitrary or hidden.
- Say no clearly and explain why, rather than avoiding the conversation or silently deprioritizing requests.

Managing a backlog with multiple stakeholders — each with their own priorities, and often limited visibility into each other's requests — is one of the most common sources of friction for a Product Owner. The core challenge isn't fitting everyone's requests in; it's maintaining a fair, transparent, and consistent process for deciding what actually gets prioritized when not everything can be done at once.

## Quick facts

- A consistent prioritization framework, applied the same way regardless of who's asking, is the foundation of managing multi-stakeholder backlog conflict fairly.
- Making the backlog and its reasoning visible reduces the perception (and reality) of favoritism or arbitrary decisions.
- This connects to [How to Say No to a Feature Request From a Stakeholder](/product-roadmap/how-to-say-no-to-a-feature-request-from-a-stakeholder).

## How to manage a multi-stakeholder backlog, step by step

1. **Establish and communicate a single, consistent prioritization framework.** Whether it's RICE, value vs. effort, or another structured method, applying it the same way to every stakeholder's request is what makes prioritization defensible and fair, rather than dependent on who asks loudest or most often.
2. **Make the backlog visible to all stakeholders**, along with enough context on priority order and reasoning. Transparency significantly reduces the perception that decisions are arbitrary or made behind closed doors.
3. **Give every stakeholder a consistent channel to submit requests**, rather than handling some through formal processes and others through hallway conversations — inconsistent intake channels create real (and perceived) unfairness.
4. **Evaluate every request against the same criteria**, tied to actual business objectives, not the requester's seniority or persistence. This is the core discipline that keeps the process fair.
5. **Communicate priority decisions and reasoning proactively**, not just when a stakeholder asks. Regular updates on what's prioritized and why prevent stakeholders from feeling ignored or out of the loop.
6. **Hold periodic cross-stakeholder alignment sessions when priorities genuinely conflict.** Sometimes stakeholders need to hear each other's competing priorities directly, rather than only hearing the PO's individual decisions.
7. **Say no clearly, with reasoning, when a request doesn't make the cut.** Avoiding the conversation, or giving a vague non-answer, usually damages trust more than a direct, well-reasoned no.
8. **Revisit and adjust the process itself if patterns of conflict persist.** Recurring tension with a specific stakeholder group might indicate the intake or communication process itself needs adjustment, not just better individual conversations.

## Why consistency matters more than any specific framework

Stakeholders generally accept not getting everything they ask for — what damages trust is a *perceived* inconsistency, where one stakeholder's request seems to get fast-tracked for reasons unrelated to actual business value (seniority, persistence, personal relationship with the PO). A consistently applied framework, even an imperfect one, tends to produce more stakeholder trust over time than an inconsistent process using a theoretically "better" framework applied unevenly.

## A worked example

A Product Owner manages a backlog fed by requests from sales, customer support, and engineering leadership — three groups with historically competing priorities. The PO establishes a single intake form all requests go through, evaluates every request using the same RICE-based scoring, and publishes a visible, prioritized backlog with brief reasoning notes for the top items. When sales pushes for an urgent feature that scores lower than currently planned work, the PO explains the specific reasoning (using the same criteria applied to every other request) rather than making an exception — and offers a clear path to revisit the request in the next planning cycle. Over time, even stakeholders who don't always get their top request prioritized report higher trust in the process, because it's consistent and transparent.

## Common mistakes when managing a multi-stakeholder backlog

- **Prioritizing based on who asks most persistently or is most senior**, rather than a consistent framework tied to real business value.
- **Keeping the backlog and reasoning hidden**, leaving stakeholders to assume decisions are arbitrary even when they aren't.
- **Avoiding direct conversations about declined requests**, letting requests quietly disappear rather than being explicitly addressed.
- **Handling different stakeholders through inconsistent intake channels**, creating real unfairness in whose requests even get properly considered.

## FAQ

**What if two senior stakeholders have directly conflicting priorities?**
Bring both perspectives together in a shared conversation using the same prioritization criteria, and if a decision still can't be reached, escalate to someone with the authority to make the final call — the PO shouldn't be left to unilaterally referee a conflict between senior stakeholders without support.

**How often should stakeholders receive updates on backlog priorities?**
Regularly and proactively, ideally at a predictable cadence (like a monthly summary), rather than only when a specific stakeholder asks — proactive communication prevents the sense of being ignored.

**Should every stakeholder request go through the same formal intake process?**
Yes, ideally — consistent intake, even for smaller or informal-seeming requests, prevents the fairness and trust issues that arise when some requests get special, less rigorous treatment.

**How do you handle a stakeholder who consistently disagrees with prioritization decisions?**
Continue applying the consistent framework and explaining the reasoning clearly — persistent disagreement despite fair, transparent reasoning may need to be escalated to leadership if it's genuinely disrupting the backlog management process.
