# What Is Product Management?

Product management is the job of deciding what a company should build next, and why. A product manager doesn't build the product themselves. Instead, they study what customers need, look at what's good for the business, and check what the engineering team can realistically build. Then they decide, out of everything the team could work on, what actually gets built first.

## Quick facts

- A product manager sits between three things: what customers need, what's good for the business, and what's technically possible to build.
- Product management is not the same as project management. A product manager decides *what* to build. A project manager makes sure it gets delivered on time and on budget.
- The main outputs of product management are a **strategy** (the big-picture plan), a **roadmap** (a timeline of what's coming), and a **backlog** (a prioritized to-do list for the team).
- Success is measured by results — like more people using the product, or more revenue — not by how many features get shipped.

## What product management actually involves

Product management is made up of four connected jobs. Each one answers a different question.

| Job | What it means | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** | Talking to users, looking at data, and testing ideas before building anything | "Is this problem real, and is it worth solving?" |
| **Strategy** | Deciding the product's overall direction | "What will we build, and what will we deliberately not build?" |
| **Prioritization** | Putting ideas in order, using tools like the [RICE framework](/product-frameworks/rice-framework-explained-with-example) | "What should we build first?" |
| **Delivery oversight** | Working with the design and engineering teams while they build, then checking if it worked | "Did this actually help?" |

A product manager usually doesn't manage the day-to-day schedule of building the product. That job — tracking deadlines, budgets, and who's doing what — often belongs to a project manager, especially in larger companies.

## Why product management exists as its own job

Before companies had product managers, decisions about what to build were often made by whoever spoke the loudest in the room — usually a senior engineer or a salesperson chasing one big deal. That's a risky way to run a product, because it means decisions get made on opinion instead of evidence.

Product management exists to fix that. It gives one person clear ownership of the "what should we build" decision, and holds that person accountable for backing it up with real evidence: user research, usage data, and a clear line back to a business goal. That's also why people sometimes call a product manager the "CEO of the product" — not because they have that much authority, but because they're expected to make trade-off decisions the way a CEO would for an entire company, just for one product.

## What good product management looks like

You can usually tell a team has strong product management when:

- They ship fewer features overall, but the features they do ship actually move a number that matters (like how many people keep using the product).
- Anyone on the team can explain *why* something is on the roadmap — not just that it's there.
- The product manager says "no" to a stakeholder's request when the data doesn't support it, even if that stakeholder is senior.
- The roadmap is treated as a plan that can change, not a fixed contract that must be followed no matter what.

## How product management connects to other jobs

Product management doesn't work alone. It connects closely to a few other roles:

- **Product marketing** takes what product management builds and decides how to explain it to the world — the messaging, the launch, the pricing story. See [What Does a Product Marketing Manager Do](/product-management/what-does-a-product-marketing-manager-do).
- **Business analysis** studies how a business currently works and documents exactly what's needed for a change — often feeding directly into a product manager's strategy at larger companies. See [What Does a Business Analyst Do](/business-analyst/what-does-a-business-analyst-do).
- **Project management** takes over once the "what and why" is decided, and manages the schedule, budget, and risk of actually delivering it. See [What Does a Project Manager Do](/project-management/what-does-a-project-manager-do).

## FAQ

**Do you need to know how to code to work in product management?**
No. Many product managers come from non-technical backgrounds, like business, design, or consulting. What matters more is being able to talk clearly with engineers, understand basic technical trade-offs, and make good decisions even when you don't have all the information. See [How to Become a Product Manager Without a Technical Background](/product-management/how-to-become-a-product-manager-without-a-technical-background).

**What's the difference between product management and product development?**
Product development is the actual process of engineers building the product. Product management is the decision-making process that happens before and alongside that — figuring out what to build and why it matters.

**Does every company need a dedicated product manager?**
Not always. In very small or early-stage companies, the founder often plays this role themselves. Companies usually hire a dedicated product manager once they have more than one product, or more than one engineering team, and someone needs to decide what each team should focus on.
