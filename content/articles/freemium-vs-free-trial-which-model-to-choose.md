# Freemium vs Free Trial: Which Model Should You Choose?

**Freemium** gives users free access to a limited version of a product, forever, with no deadline — the limitation is in *features or usage*, not time. A **free trial** gives users full, unrestricted access to the product for a limited *time period*, after which they must pay to keep using it at all. The core difference: freemium limits what you get; a free trial limits how long you get it. Choosing between them depends heavily on how quickly your product proves its value, and how naturally it spreads through word of mouth.

## Quick facts

- **Freemium:** limited features or usage, available indefinitely, for free.
- **Free trial:** full features, available for a limited time (commonly 7, 14, or 30 days), then requires payment.
- Freemium tends to work best for products with fast, easy-to-experience value and strong viral or word-of-mouth potential.
- Free trials tend to work best for products where value takes longer to experience, or where usage naturally has a clear evaluation period.
- Both models are common tools within a broader [product-led growth](/product-led-growth/what-is-product-led-growth-plg) strategy.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | Freemium | Free Trial |
|---|---|---|
| What's limited | Features or usage volume | Time |
| Duration | Indefinite | Fixed (commonly 7-30 days) |
| Conversion trigger | User hits a usage limit or wants a locked feature | Trial period ends |
| Works best for | Products with fast value and viral/network effects | Products needing more time to demonstrate full value |
| Common examples | Communication tools, note-taking apps, cloud storage | Enterprise software, tools requiring setup or team onboarding |
| Risk | Free users may never feel pressure to upgrade | Users may not experience enough value within the trial window to convert |

## When freemium works well

Freemium tends to succeed when a product can deliver real, standalone value even within its limited free version — enough that users genuinely want to keep using it, and enough that some meaningful portion naturally want to upgrade once they hit a limit or want an unlocked feature. It also works particularly well for products with strong viral or network effects, since a large free user base can itself become a growth engine — more free users using and inviting others expands the top of the funnel, even before any of them ever pay.

## When a free trial works better

A free trial makes more sense when a product's real value takes longer to experience, or requires deeper usage to fully evaluate — common in enterprise or workflow-heavy software, where a user needs time to integrate the tool into their existing process before deciding if it's worth paying for. A time-limited trial also creates a natural sense of urgency that freemium, with no deadline at all, doesn't naturally provide — this urgency can actually help conversion for products where users might otherwise use a free tier indefinitely without ever seriously considering paying.

## The core trade-off: urgency vs continuous exposure

Freemium optimizes for **long-term, low-pressure exposure** — free users stick around, become familiar with the product, and might convert whenever they eventually hit a real limitation. A free trial optimizes for **urgency** — a countdown creates real pressure to decide now, which can drive faster conversion decisions, but also means users who haven't reached a clear value moment by the deadline are likely to simply leave, with no ongoing relationship to the product afterward.

## Can a company use both?

Yes, and some companies do combine them — offering a full-access free trial first (to demonstrate maximum value quickly), which then downgrades to a limited freemium tier if the user doesn't convert, rather than cutting off access entirely. This hybrid approach tries to capture the best of both: the urgency of a trial deadline, plus the ongoing exposure and word-of-mouth potential of a freemium tier for those who don't convert immediately.

## How to actually decide between them

Ask two honest questions about your specific product: first, how quickly can a new user experience real, meaningful value — if it's fast (minutes to a few days), freemium's limited-but-immediate value proposition tends to work well; if it's slower (requiring team setup, data import, or a longer workflow), a trial's full-access approach gives users the space needed to get there. Second, does your product benefit meaningfully from a large base of free, non-paying users — through virality, network effects, or simply brand visibility — or does supporting free users at scale mostly just cost you money with little additional benefit? Products with strong network effects usually lean toward freemium; products without them often do better with a trial, since indefinitely supporting non-paying free users provides less strategic value.

## Common mistakes with each model

- **Freemium: setting the free tier too generous.** If the free tier already satisfies most users completely, there's little reason for anyone to ever upgrade.
- **Freemium: setting the free tier too limited.** If it's too restrictive, users never experience enough real value to want to pay for more, and simply leave instead.
- **Free trial: making the trial too short for the product's actual value curve.** A 7-day trial for a product that takes two weeks of real use to show its full value sets users up to churn before ever seeing the payoff.
- **Free trial: requiring a credit card upfront, which reduces trial signups but can improve trial-to-paid conversion.** This is a real trade-off worth deliberately testing, not defaulting into without consideration — see [How to Design a Self-Serve Onboarding Funnel](/product-led-growth/how-to-design-a-self-serve-onboarding-funnel).

## FAQ

**Which model converts better, freemium or a free trial?**
Neither is universally better — free trials often have a higher percentage conversion rate among people who sign up, since there's more urgency, while freemium often brings in a much larger total volume of users, some of whom convert over a longer period. The right comparison depends on your specific product and business model, not a general rule.

**Can a product switch from one model to the other later?**
Yes, and companies do this — a shift usually reflects a better understanding of how quickly the product demonstrates value, or a change in business priorities, like wanting more urgency in the conversion funnel or wanting a larger top-of-funnel user base for network effects.

**Is freemium always cheaper to run than a free trial?**
Not necessarily — while freemium avoids the cost of building a trial-expiration and billing-transition flow, it can create meaningful ongoing infrastructure and support costs for a large base of free users who never pay, especially at scale.

**Do B2B and B2C companies typically choose differently between these models?**
There's a common pattern where B2C products with strong viral potential often lean toward freemium, while B2B products, especially ones requiring team setup or a longer evaluation process, often lean toward free trials — but this is a general tendency, not a strict rule, and plenty of successful companies in each category use the other model.
