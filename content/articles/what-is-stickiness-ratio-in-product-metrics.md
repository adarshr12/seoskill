# What Is Stickiness Ratio in Product Metrics?

Stickiness ratio measures how habitually people actually use your product, calculated by dividing Daily Active Users by Monthly Active Users. It's the same calculation as [DAU/MAU ratio](/product-metrics/what-is-daumau-ratio-and-why-it-matters) — "stickiness" is simply the more descriptive, commonly used name for what the ratio is actually trying to capture: how much a product has become a regular, "sticky" habit versus something used only occasionally.

## Quick facts

- The formula: **Stickiness ratio = Daily Active Users ÷ Monthly Active Users × 100**
- "Stickiness ratio" and "DAU/MAU ratio" refer to the exact same metric.
- A higher stickiness ratio means users are engaging more frequently relative to their overall monthly presence.
- What counts as a "good" stickiness ratio varies enormously depending on how frequently a product is naturally meant to be used.

## Why "stickiness" is a useful way to frame this metric

While "DAU/MAU ratio" describes the calculation, "stickiness" describes what the number actually represents in plain terms — how much the product has become embedded in a user's regular routine, versus something they use occasionally and could easily forget about or abandon. This framing is often more intuitive when discussing the metric with non-technical stakeholders, since it directly names the underlying business concern (is this product becoming a genuine habit) rather than just the mechanical calculation behind it.

## What drives stickiness up or down

| Factor | Effect on stickiness |
|---|---|
| Strong core habit loop (a clear reason to return daily) | Increases stickiness |
| Notifications and reminders that genuinely add value | Can increase stickiness, if not overused to the point of annoyance |
| A product naturally used infrequently by design | Lower stickiness is expected and not necessarily a problem |
| Weak onboarding that fails to establish a habit early | Decreases stickiness, since users never form the habit in the first place |
| Strong network effects (more useful as more people use it) | Tends to increase stickiness over time as the network grows |

## How to actually improve stickiness

Improving stickiness usually comes down to strengthening the core reason someone has to return regularly — this might mean surfacing genuinely relevant, timely content or updates, building features that create natural recurring touchpoints (like notifications about a teammate's activity), or simply improving the core product experience enough that people want to return on their own, without needing external prompts. It's worth being cautious about manufacturing artificial stickiness through excessive notifications or dark patterns — this can temporarily boost the ratio while damaging long-term trust and genuine retention, which ultimately hurts the business more than it helps.

## Why stickiness ratio needs context to interpret

A stickiness ratio of 10% might be a real concern for a messaging app meant to be used many times daily, but entirely healthy for a monthly expense-reporting tool that people are only supposed to need once or twice a month. Before treating a stickiness number as good or bad, it's essential to first ask what usage frequency the product actually requires to deliver its core value — comparing against direct competitors with a similar natural usage pattern is far more meaningful than comparing against a generic, unrelated benchmark.

## Common mistakes when using stickiness ratio

- **Chasing a universally "good" stickiness number without considering your product's natural usage cadence.** As covered above, the right target varies enormously by product type.
- **Trying to artificially inflate stickiness through excessive notifications rather than genuine value.** This risks damaging user trust and long-term retention for a short-term metric boost.
- **Only looking at the blended, overall stickiness number** rather than segmenting by user type or cohort, which can hide meaningfully different engagement patterns across your user base.
- **Not tracking stickiness trends over time**, missing whether a product change genuinely improved habitual usage or just shifted when people happen to check in.

## FAQ

**Is stickiness ratio the same as DAU/MAU ratio?**
Yes — they're the exact same calculation and metric, just described with different names. "Stickiness" is the more descriptive, plain-language term; "DAU/MAU ratio" is the more literal, formula-based name.

**What's considered a good stickiness ratio?**
It varies enormously by product type — 10-20% is common for many products, while top habitual consumer apps can reach 50%+. The right number depends heavily on how frequently your specific product is naturally meant to be used, not a universal benchmark.

**Can stickiness ratio be calculated for a specific feature, not just the whole product?**
Yes — the same DAU/MAU calculation can be applied to a specific feature's daily versus monthly active users, revealing which parts of a product have become genuinely habitual versus occasionally used.

**Does higher stickiness always mean a healthier product?**
Generally yes when it reflects genuine value delivery, but it's worth distinguishing real, value-driven stickiness from artificially inflated engagement caused by manipulative notification tactics, which can hurt trust and long-term retention even while boosting the short-term number.
