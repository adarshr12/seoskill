# Requirement Elicitation Techniques (Interviews, Workshops, Surveys)

Requirement elicitation is the process of gathering the information needed to understand what a project or system actually needs to do — and different techniques (interviews, workshops, surveys, observation, and document analysis) each work better for different situations. Most real projects use a combination of these, not just one.

## Quick facts

- No single elicitation technique surfaces every relevant requirement — combining methods produces a more complete, accurate picture.
- The right technique depends on how many stakeholders are involved, how much detail is needed, and how much time is available.
- This connects to [How to Run a Requirements Gathering Workshop](/business-analyst/how-to-run-a-requirements-gathering-workshop), a deeper dive on one specific technique below.

## Common elicitation techniques compared

| Technique | Best for | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| One-on-one interviews | Deep, detailed understanding from a single stakeholder | Time-intensive; doesn't scale to many stakeholders efficiently |
| Workshops | Building shared alignment across multiple stakeholders at once | Requires strong facilitation; can be dominated by vocal participants |
| Surveys | Gathering input from a large number of people efficiently | Less depth than interviews; can't ask real-time follow-up questions |
| Direct observation | Understanding how a process actually works in practice, not just how people describe it | Time-intensive; requires access to observe real work happening |
| Document analysis | Understanding existing processes, systems, or historical decisions | Documents can be outdated or incomplete |

## One-on-one interviews

Interviews work best when you need deep, nuanced understanding from a specific stakeholder, especially one with unique or critical expertise. They allow real-time follow-up questions and let the interviewer dig into ambiguous or surprising answers immediately, which structured surveys can't do. The trade-off is time — interviews don't scale efficiently to gathering input from dozens of stakeholders.

## Workshops

Workshops bring multiple stakeholders together to build shared understanding and surface disagreements directly, in real time — valuable when different stakeholders have genuinely different perspectives that need to be reconciled. They require strong facilitation to work well (see the [dedicated guide](/business-analyst/how-to-run-a-requirements-gathering-workshop)), since without it, a workshop risks being dominated by the most vocal participants.

## Surveys

Surveys efficiently gather input from a large number of stakeholders or users, useful when you need broad, quantifiable signal rather than deep individual understanding. Their main limitation is the inability to ask real-time follow-up questions — an ambiguous or surprising survey response can't be immediately clarified the way it could in an interview.

## Direct observation

Observing how people actually perform a process, rather than only asking them to describe it, often reveals real-world workarounds, inefficiencies, or details that people don't think to mention when simply asked — sometimes because a workaround has become so habitual they no longer consciously notice it. This technique is especially valuable for understanding existing processes accurately (see [What Is Process Mapping in Business Analysis](/business-analyst/what-is-process-mapping-in-business-analysis)).

## Document analysis

Reviewing existing documentation — past requirements, system documentation, policy documents, historical decisions — provides useful background context before engaging stakeholders directly, and can reveal historical reasoning behind current processes that stakeholders themselves may not fully remember or understand.

## How to choose and combine techniques

Most real requirements-gathering efforts combine several techniques rather than relying on just one: document analysis and observation to build initial context, interviews with key stakeholders for deep understanding, a workshop to build shared alignment across the group, and possibly a survey if broader input from many people (like end users) is needed. The right combination depends on the number of stakeholders, the complexity of the requirements, and the time available.

## Common mistakes with requirement elicitation

- **Relying on only one technique** when the situation genuinely calls for a combination — a single interview, for example, might miss context a document review or observation would have revealed.
- **Skipping document review and observation**, jumping straight to asking stakeholders, and missing context that would have made those conversations more informed and efficient.
- **Using a survey when deep, nuanced understanding is actually needed**, losing the follow-up questioning that a real conversation would allow.
- **Running an unfacilitated workshop**, letting the most vocal participants dominate rather than surfacing genuinely representative input.

## FAQ

**Which elicitation technique is most commonly used?**
Interviews and workshops are among the most commonly used for gathering detailed requirements, though the best choice always depends on the specific project's stakeholder structure and needs.

**How many elicitation techniques should a single project use?**
Most substantial projects benefit from combining at least two or three techniques — relying on just one often leaves gaps that a complementary method would have filled.

**Is direct observation always possible?**
No — it requires access to observe the actual process in action, which isn't always feasible (for example, with highly confidential or physically inaccessible processes), in which case interviews and document analysis become more central.

**How do you choose between an interview and a workshop for a specific stakeholder group?**
Interviews work better for deep, individual understanding or when stakeholders have significantly different perspectives that shouldn't be discussed together yet; workshops work better once you want to build shared alignment or resolve disagreements directly among the group.
