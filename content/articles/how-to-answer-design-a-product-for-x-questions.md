# How to Answer "Design a Product for X" Questions

**TL;DR**
- Clarify the target user and their specific problem before proposing any solution.
- Generate a few different solution directions before committing to one, showing genuine breadth of thinking.
- Prioritize which features matter most within your chosen solution, rather than listing everything you can think of.

"Design a product for X" questions — "design a product for blind people," "design an alarm clock for kids" — ask a candidate to work through a full mini product design process live: understanding a user, identifying their real needs, and proposing a thoughtfully prioritized solution. Interviewers use this format to observe genuine product thinking, not to find one specific "correct" product idea.

## Quick facts

- Strong answers spend real time understanding the user before jumping to solutions.
- Generating multiple solution directions, then choosing and justifying one, demonstrates stronger thinking than presenting a single idea immediately.
- This connects to [How to Answer a Case Study Interview Question](/pm-interview-prep/how-to-answer-a-case-study-interview-question), sharing a similar structured-reasoning approach.

## How to answer "design a product for X" questions, step by step

1. **Clarify who the user is and what context matters.** "Design a product for X" is often intentionally broad — asking a clarifying question (age range, specific use context, existing constraints) narrows the problem to something you can meaningfully design for.
2. **Identify the user's real underlying needs, not just surface wants.** Spend genuine time thinking through what this specific user actually struggles with — this step often gets rushed, but it's what separates a thoughtful answer from a generic one.
3. **Generate a few different solution directions before committing to one.** Briefly naming 2-3 different possible approaches, even if you'll only develop one in depth, demonstrates breadth of thinking rather than anchoring immediately on the first idea that comes to mind.
4. **Choose one direction and justify why it's the strongest.** Explain your reasoning for selecting this approach over the others you considered — this reasoning is often more valuable to the interviewer than the specific choice itself.
5. **Describe the core solution and its key features.** Focus on the features that most directly address the user's core need, rather than listing every possible feature you can imagine.
6. **Prioritize the features you've proposed.** Identify which is most essential to build first (a true minimum viable version) versus which could come later — this demonstrates real product judgment, not just creative brainstorming.
7. **Consider trade-offs, risks, or edge cases briefly.** Acknowledging a genuine limitation or open question about your proposed solution shows realistic, grounded thinking rather than an idealized pitch.
8. **Summarize your recommendation clearly.** End with a concise restatement of your core solution and why it addresses the user's actual need, tying the answer back together.

## Why understanding the user matters more than the specific product idea

The strength of an answer to this question comes far more from how well you understand and articulate the target user's actual needs than from the cleverness of the specific product idea you propose. A well-reasoned, even relatively simple solution built on genuine user understanding will outperform a flashy, creative idea that skips real consideration of who the user is and what they actually need.

## A worked example

**Prompt:** "Design a product for elderly people living alone."

A strong answer clarifies the specific context first: "Are we thinking about elderly people who are generally healthy and independent, or those with more significant mobility or health concerns?" After clarifying (say, generally healthy but living alone), the candidate identifies core needs: safety/emergency response, social connection (isolation is a common issue), and maintaining independence without feeling monitored or infantilized. They briefly consider a few directions — a wearable emergency alert device, a check-in app for family, a simplified communication tool — before choosing to focus on a check-in system that balances safety with dignity: a simple daily check-in prompt that alerts a family member only if missed, rather than continuous monitoring that could feel invasive. They then prioritize the core check-in feature as the true MVP, with social features (video calls, community connections) as a valuable later addition, and note the trade-off between safety and the user's sense of independence as an open design tension worth testing with real users.

## Common mistakes when answering design questions

- **Jumping straight to a solution without genuinely understanding the user first**, missing the foundational thinking interviewers are evaluating.
- **Proposing only one idea without considering alternatives**, missing the chance to demonstrate breadth of thinking.
- **Listing many features without prioritizing them**, rather than clearly identifying what's most essential to build first.
- **Ignoring realistic trade-offs or constraints**, presenting an idealized solution that doesn't acknowledge genuine limitations or open questions.

## FAQ

**How much time should I spend understanding the user versus proposing solutions?**
Roughly a third to half of your total answer time is reasonable for understanding the user and their needs — rushing this part to get to solutions faster usually produces a weaker overall answer.

**Is it okay to choose an unconventional or creative solution?**
Yes, as long as it's genuinely justified by real user understanding — creativity is valued, but only when it's grounded in solving an actual identified need, not creativity for its own sake.

**Should I mention business considerations, or just focus on the user?**
Briefly acknowledging business viability (how the product might generate value or fit a business model) can strengthen your answer, though the primary focus should remain on genuine user understanding and problem-solving.

**What if the interviewer pushes back on my proposed solution?**
Engage genuinely with the pushback and consider adjusting your thinking if it raises a valid point — this demonstrates flexibility and real reasoning, rather than defensively sticking with your original idea regardless of new information.
