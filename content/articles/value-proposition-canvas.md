# The Value Proposition Canvas Explained

The Value Proposition Canvas, created by Alexander Osterwalder as a companion tool to the [Business Model Canvas](/product-frameworks/business-model-canvas-explained), zooms in specifically on the fit between what customers actually need and what your product actually offers. It splits into two sides: a **Customer Profile** (the customer's jobs, pains, and gains) and a **Value Map** (your product's pain relievers, gain creators, and products/services) — and the goal is checking whether these two sides genuinely line up, rather than assuming they do.

## Quick facts

- The canvas has two halves: the **Customer Profile** (jobs, pains, gains) and the **Value Map** (pain relievers, gain creators, products/services).
- It's designed to make the fit — or lack of fit — between customer needs and your offering explicit and visible, rather than assumed.
- It works well alongside [Jobs to Be Done](/product-frameworks/jobs-to-be-done-framework), since "customer jobs" draws directly on that same underlying idea.
- Created by Alexander Osterwalder as a more detailed, customer-focused companion to the Business Model Canvas.

## The two halves, explained

| Side | Component | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer Profile** | Jobs | What the customer is actually trying to get done |
| | Pains | The frustrations, obstacles, and risks they experience |
| | Gains | The outcomes and benefits they want |
| **Value Map** | Products/Services | What you actually offer |
| | Pain Relievers | How your offering specifically reduces the customer's pains |
| | Gain Creators | How your offering specifically produces the customer's desired gains |

## A worked example: a small business accounting tool

**Customer Profile:**
- **Jobs:** Track income and expenses, prepare for tax season, understand if the business is actually profitable.
- **Pains:** Spreadsheets are error-prone and time-consuming; unsure if books are "tax-ready"; anxiety around tax season.
- **Gains:** Confidence the numbers are accurate; time saved versus manual tracking; peace of mind heading into tax season.

**Value Map:**
- **Products/Services:** Automated bookkeeping software with bank account syncing and tax-category tagging.
- **Pain Relievers:** Automatic transaction categorization removes manual data entry error risk; built-in tax-readiness checklist removes tax-season anxiety.
- **Gain Creators:** Real-time profitability dashboard delivers the "am I actually making money" confidence customers want.

Laid out this way, it's easy to check the fit directly: does each customer pain have a corresponding pain reliever, and does each desired gain have a corresponding gain creator? If a major customer pain has no clear pain reliever in the Value Map, that's a visible, specific gap — either the product needs to address it, or the messaging needs to stop implying that it does.

## Why checking "fit" explicitly matters

It's common for a team to assume their product addresses real customer needs without ever laying out the specific connection point by point. The Value Proposition Canvas forces this connection to be explicit — for every pain and gain identified on the customer side, there should be a specific, identifiable element on the product side addressing it. When a gap shows up (a real customer pain with no corresponding pain reliever), that's either a genuine product gap worth addressing, or a sign the product's real value proposition needs to be redefined more honestly around what it actually does well.

## How this connects to Jobs to Be Done

The "Jobs" component of the Customer Profile draws directly on the [Jobs to Be Done](/product-frameworks/jobs-to-be-done-framework) framework's core idea — customers aren't just buying features, they're hiring a solution to accomplish something in their life or work. Filling out the Jobs section well often benefits from the same kind of deep, situation-specific customer understanding that a JTBD-style interview surfaces, rather than a superficial guess at what customers are "trying to do."

## Common mistakes when using the Value Proposition Canvas

- **Filling out the Value Map first, then reverse-engineering a Customer Profile to match.** This defeats the purpose — the canvas is meant to genuinely test fit, not confirm an existing assumption about the product.
- **Writing vague, generic entries** ("save time," "reduce stress") that could apply to almost any product, rather than specific, real pains and gains grounded in genuine customer understanding.
- **Only listing pains and gains you already address well**, missing real customer pains your product doesn't yet solve — the value of the exercise comes specifically from surfacing gaps, not just confirming existing strengths.
- **Treating it as a one-time exercise rather than revisiting it as customer understanding deepens.** Like most discovery tools, its value comes from staying current with real, evolving customer understanding.

## FAQ

**Who created the Value Proposition Canvas?**
Alexander Osterwalder, the same creator of the Business Model Canvas, developed it as a more detailed, zoomed-in companion tool focused specifically on the customer-product fit within one block of that broader canvas.

**How is this different from the Business Model Canvas?**
The [Business Model Canvas](/product-frameworks/business-model-canvas-explained) covers the entire business model across nine blocks. The Value Proposition Canvas zooms in specifically on two of those blocks — Customer Segments and Value Propositions — with much more detail on the customer-product fit specifically.

**Should the Customer Profile be based on research, or can it be a team's best guess?**
It should be grounded in real customer research wherever possible — a Customer Profile built purely on internal assumption risks reflecting what the team believes customers want rather than what they actually experience, defeating the purpose of the exercise.

**Can a product have more than one Value Proposition Canvas?**
Yes — if a product serves genuinely distinct customer segments with different jobs, pains, and gains, building a separate canvas for each segment often reveals more useful, specific insight than trying to force one generic canvas to represent everyone.
