# What Is a Product Owner?

A **product owner** is the person on a Scrum team accountable for maximizing the value of the product by managing and prioritizing the product backlog. The product owner decides what the team builds next, writes and refines user stories, sets acceptance criteria, and is the single point of authority for saying a piece of work is "done." It's one of three formal roles defined by the Scrum Guide, alongside the Scrum Master and the development team.

## Quick facts

- One of three official Scrum roles: **Product Owner, Scrum Master, Development Team**
- Owns the **product backlog** — order, content, and clarity
- Is a single person, not a committee — Scrum requires one accountable backlog owner
- Different from a product manager: a PO typically manages one team's backlog; a PM often sets strategy across multiple teams
- In SAFe, "Product Owner" is a distinct team-level role from "Product Manager" (ART-level) — see [SAFe roles explained](/safe/safe-roles-explained-rte-vs-product-manager-vs-product-owner-vs-scrum-master)

## What a product owner does day to day

| Responsibility | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Backlog management | Keeps the [product backlog](/agile-scrum-safe/how-to-write-and-manage-a-product-backlog) ordered by priority and continuously refined |
| Writing user stories | Translates requirements into stories with clear [acceptance criteria](/product-requirements/how-to-write-acceptance-criteria-for-user-stories) |
| Sprint planning | Presents the top of the backlog and answers clarifying questions so the team can commit to a sprint goal |
| Stakeholder liaison | Represents the voice of the customer/business to the dev team, and represents the team's progress back to stakeholders |
| Accepting work | Reviews completed stories against acceptance criteria and decides if they meet [Definition of Done](/agile-scrum-safe/glossary/what-is-definition-of-done-in-agile) |

## Product owner vs. product manager

The two roles are often confused because both prioritize what gets built — but they operate at different altitudes. A product manager typically owns multi-quarter strategy and may oversee several teams or a whole product line; a product owner owns one team's backlog at the sprint level, translating that strategy into ready-to-build work. In small companies, one person often does both. Full comparison: [Product Manager vs Project Manager vs Product Owner](/product-management/product-manager-vs-project-manager-vs-product-owner).

## Skills a good product owner needs

- **Prioritization judgment** — able to defend backlog order with reasoning, not just gut feel
- **Clear written communication** — ambiguous user stories are the single biggest cause of rework in Scrum teams
- **Domain knowledge** — enough understanding of the business/customer problem to make fast calls without escalating every decision
- **Comfort saying no** — protecting the sprint from scope creep and stakeholder pressure mid-sprint

## How to become a product owner

Most product owners come from a business analyst, PM, or domain-expert background rather than an engineering one. A **Certified Scrum Product Owner (CSPO)** or **Professional Scrum Product Owner (PSPO)** certification is the most common credential — see our take on [whether CSPO is worth it](/agile-scrum-safe/certified-scrum-product-owner-cspo-is-it-worth-it). It's also a common stepping stone into a full product manager role.

## FAQ

**Can a product owner also be the Scrum Master?**
No — the Scrum Guide treats these as separate accountabilities to avoid conflicts of interest (the PO wants more scope in, the Scrum Master protects the team's capacity). Some smaller organizations blur this in practice, but it's not the intended design.

**Does a product owner need to be technical?**
No, but they need enough technical literacy to have informed conversations with engineers about trade-offs and estimate feasibility — not to write code themselves.

**Is product owner a good entry point into product management?**
Yes — it's one of the more accessible paths into product roles because it doesn't require a business-strategy background, just strong prioritization and communication skills within a defined domain.
