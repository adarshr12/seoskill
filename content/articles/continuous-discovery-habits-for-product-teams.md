# Continuous Discovery Habits for Product Teams

Continuous discovery, a concept popularized by Teresa Torres, means talking to customers on a regular, ongoing basis — often weekly — rather than doing research only in occasional, large pushes before a big decision. The core belief: product teams that maintain regular, small touchpoints with real customers develop sharper intuition and catch problems earlier than teams that only research sporadically, right before a major initiative.

## Quick facts

- The core habit is **weekly customer touchpoints** — small, regular conversations, not occasional large research projects.
- Continuous discovery pairs closely with the [Opportunity Solution Tree](/product-frameworks/opportunity-solution-tree-explained), which gets updated as ongoing discovery surfaces new understanding.
- The goal is building sustained customer intuition across the whole team, not just running research as a one-off project phase.
- Popularized by Teresa Torres, a well-known product discovery coach.

## Why weekly touchpoints matter more than occasional big research pushes

Occasional, large research projects tend to happen only before major decisions, meaning a team's customer understanding can become stale between those pushes — priorities and customer needs shift continuously, but the team's knowledge doesn't update unless another big research effort is deliberately scheduled. Weekly touchpoints, even small ones, keep customer understanding continuously fresh, letting a team catch shifting needs and emerging problems much earlier than they would through periodic, large-batch research alone.

## The core continuous discovery habits

| Habit | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **Weekly customer conversations** | A regular cadence of interviews or touchpoints, even just one per week, rather than sporadic research bursts |
| **Small, focused research questions** | Each conversation doesn't need to cover everything — a specific, narrow question per touchpoint is often more useful |
| **Updating the opportunity solution tree regularly** | New understanding from ongoing conversations feeds back into a living, evolving picture, not a static one-time artifact |
| **Involving the whole team, not just researchers** | Product managers, designers, and sometimes engineers directly participate in customer conversations, not just a dedicated research function |
| **Synthesizing findings quickly and sharing broadly** | Insight from each conversation gets captured and shared promptly, rather than sitting unprocessed |

## Why involving the whole team matters

A common pattern in occasional, project-based research is that findings get filtered through a dedicated researcher or a single product manager, then summarized for the rest of the team — losing nuance and immediacy in the process. Continuous discovery encourages broader team involvement in actual customer conversations, on the theory that direct exposure builds much stronger, more durable intuition than reading someone else's research summary — a designer who's personally heard a customer's frustration firsthand tends to design around it more effectively than one who's only read about it secondhand.

## How to actually build this habit on a team that doesn't have it yet

Starting continuous discovery doesn't require an elaborate research program from day one — a realistic starting point is committing to just one customer conversation per week, rotating who conducts it across the team, with a simple, consistent process for quickly capturing and sharing what was learned. Building the habit itself matters more initially than optimizing the process — a consistent, if imperfect, weekly cadence beats an occasional, more elaborate research effort that doesn't sustain itself over time.

## How continuous discovery connects to prioritization

Regular, ongoing customer contact directly feeds into better-informed prioritization decisions — a team that's continuously talking to customers has fresher, more current evidence to weigh when making calls with frameworks like [RICE](/product-frameworks/rice-framework-explained-with-example), rather than relying on research that may be many months old and increasingly disconnected from current customer reality. This is part of why continuous discovery and the [Opportunity Solution Tree](/product-frameworks/opportunity-solution-tree-explained) are often taught together — the tree is the living artifact that continuous discovery keeps updated and accurate.

## Common mistakes when building continuous discovery habits

- **Treating it as a project with a defined end, rather than an ongoing team habit.** The value comes specifically from sustained, continuous practice, not a temporary initiative that fades after a few months.
- **Only having a dedicated researcher conduct customer conversations**, missing the broader team's direct exposure and the intuition it builds.
- **Trying to cover too much in each weekly touchpoint**, rather than keeping individual conversations focused on a specific, narrow question.
- **Not maintaining a living artifact (like an opportunity solution tree) to actually capture and use the accumulating insight.** Without this, ongoing conversations risk generating insight that's never systematically captured or acted on.

## FAQ

**How much time does continuous discovery actually require each week?**
It varies, but many teams start with as little as one 30-45 minute customer conversation per week, plus a short synthesis session — a modest, sustainable time commitment rather than a significant new project.

**Is continuous discovery only relevant for B2C products with many users to talk to?**
No — it applies to B2B products too, though B2B teams often need to be more deliberate about maintaining a rotating pool of willing customer contacts, given typically smaller customer bases than consumer products.

**Does continuous discovery replace the need for larger, occasional research projects?**
Not entirely — larger, more structured research efforts still have value for deeper, more comprehensive questions; continuous discovery complements this by keeping the team's baseline understanding fresh between those larger efforts, rather than replacing them entirely.

**Who should be responsible for scheduling and running weekly customer conversations?**
Practices vary, but many teams rotate this responsibility across product, design, and sometimes engineering, rather than assigning it permanently to one person — this distributes the direct customer exposure and its intuition-building benefit more broadly across the team.
