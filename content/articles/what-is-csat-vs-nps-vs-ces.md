# What Is CSAT vs NPS vs CES?

**CSAT**, **NPS**, and **CES** are the three most common customer experience metrics, each measuring something genuinely different: **CSAT** (Customer Satisfaction) measures satisfaction with one specific interaction, **NPS** (Net Promoter Score) measures overall loyalty and likelihood to recommend, and **CES** (Customer Effort Score) measures how much effort it took to get something done. Using the right one for the right situation — and understanding that they're not interchangeable — matters more than picking a single "best" metric.

## Quick facts

- **CSAT:** "How satisfied were you with [X]?" — measures satisfaction with one specific interaction or transaction.
- **NPS:** "How likely are you to recommend us?" — measures overall loyalty and brand sentiment. See [full NPS guide](/product-metrics/what-is-nps-and-how-to-calculate-it).
- **CES:** "How easy was it to [accomplish X]?" — measures effort required, especially common after a support interaction.
- Each is asked at a different moment and answers a different question — none is a universal replacement for the others.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | CSAT | NPS | CES |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question | "How satisfied were you with X?" | "How likely are you to recommend us?" | "How easy was it to resolve/complete X?" |
| Scale | Typically 1-5 or 1-10 | 0-10 | Typically 1-5 or 1-7 |
| Measures | Satisfaction with a specific moment | Overall loyalty and brand sentiment | Effort required to accomplish something |
| Best timing | Right after a specific interaction | Periodic, relationship-level check-in | Right after a support or service interaction |
| Best for | Evaluating one specific touchpoint's quality | Tracking overall brand/product health | Evaluating support/service process quality |

## When to use CSAT

CSAT is best for evaluating a specific, bounded interaction — right after a purchase, a support ticket resolution, or a specific in-app action. Because it's tied to one moment, it's useful for pinpointing exactly which touchpoints in a customer journey are working well and which aren't, in a way a broader, less frequent metric like NPS can't isolate as precisely.

## When to use NPS

[NPS](/product-metrics/what-is-nps-and-how-to-calculate-it) is best for tracking overall brand or product health over time — a periodic, relationship-level check-in rather than a reaction to one specific moment. It's the metric most commonly reported to leadership as a single, memorable number representing overall customer sentiment, though as covered in the full NPS guide, it works best paired with a follow-up "why" question to be genuinely actionable.

## When to use CES

CES is specifically useful for evaluating how much friction or effort a process required — most commonly used right after a support interaction, but also applicable to any specific task, like completing checkout or setting up an account. Research behind CES suggests that reducing customer effort is often a stronger driver of loyalty than trying to delight customers with above-and-beyond service — a low-effort experience tends to build more loyalty than a high-effort one, even if the high-effort one ultimately gets resolved successfully.

## A worked example showing all three together

Picture a software company after a customer contacts support about a billing issue:

- **CES** (asked immediately after the support interaction): "How easy was it to resolve your issue today?" — measures the specific support experience's effort level.
- **CSAT** (asked shortly after): "How satisfied were you with this support interaction?" — measures satisfaction with that specific interaction overall.
- **NPS** (asked periodically, unrelated to this specific interaction): "How likely are you to recommend us to a colleague?" — measures the customer's overall relationship sentiment, which this one support interaction is just one input into, among many others over time.

Using all three appropriately gives a fuller picture: a customer might report a difficult support interaction (low CES) that was still ultimately resolved satisfactorily (moderate CSAT), while their overall loyalty (NPS) remains high due to their broader positive experience with the product overall — three genuinely different signals from the same underlying customer relationship.

## Common mistakes when using these metrics

- **Using NPS to evaluate a specific interaction**, when it's designed to measure overall relationship sentiment — this produces a poorly timed, less actionable signal.
- **Treating CSAT scores as a measure of overall loyalty**, when they only reflect satisfaction with one specific, bounded moment.
- **Ignoring CES, assuming CSAT alone covers effort.** A customer can report reasonable satisfaction with an outcome while the process to get there was genuinely high-effort — CES captures this distinctly.
- **Surveying too frequently with all three metrics**, causing survey fatigue that reduces response rates and data quality across the board.

## FAQ

**Should a company track all three metrics, or pick just one?**
Most mature customer experience programs track a combination, since each answers a genuinely different question — the right combination depends on your specific business, but relying on just one metric alone typically leaves a real gap in understanding.

**Which metric best predicts customer churn?**
Research suggests CES (effort) is often a strong predictor of dissatisfaction and churn risk following a service interaction, though NPS is more commonly used as a general, ongoing churn-risk indicator at the relationship level — using them together provides a stronger signal than either alone.

**Is CSAT the same as star ratings on a review site?**
They're conceptually similar — both measure satisfaction with something specific — but CSAT is typically collected directly by the company as part of a structured measurement program, while public review ratings are collected by a third-party platform and serve a different, more public purpose.

**How often should CES be measured?**
CES is best measured immediately following a specific interaction it's meant to evaluate (like right after a support ticket closes), rather than as a periodic, standalone survey unrelated to a specific recent interaction — timing close to the actual event produces more accurate, reliable responses.
