# How to Conduct Effective User Interviews

A user interview is a one-on-one conversation with a real or potential customer, designed to understand their actual problems, behavior, and needs — not to pitch them your product or confirm what you already believe. Done well, it's one of the most powerful tools in product management and business analysis. Done poorly, with leading questions or a rushed structure, it produces answers that just confirm existing assumptions instead of revealing anything new.

## Quick facts

- The goal of a user interview is to understand real behavior and problems, not to test whether someone likes your idea.
- Open-ended questions ("walk me through the last time you...") reveal far more than closed, leading questions ("wouldn't a dashboard help you?").
- Asking about specific past behavior is more reliable than asking someone to predict their future behavior — people are often wrong about what they'd hypothetically do.
- 5-8 well-run interviews with the right people often reveal more useful insight than 50 rushed or poorly structured ones.
- This connects directly to the [Jobs to Be Done interview technique](/product-discovery/jobs-to-be-done-interview-technique), a more specific style of interview focused on uncovering the real "job" behind a decision.

## How to conduct a user interview, step by step

1. **Define what you're actually trying to learn before scheduling anything.** A vague goal like "learn about our users" produces a vague, unfocused conversation — a specific goal like "understand why people abandon setup halfway through" produces a much sharper, more useful interview.
2. **Recruit the right people, not just anyone available.** If you're studying a specific problem, talk to people who've actually experienced it recently, not a random mix of users regardless of relevance.
3. **Write a loose guide, not a rigid script.** Prepare a handful of open-ended starting questions, but stay flexible enough to follow an interesting, unexpected answer instead of rigidly sticking to your prepared list.
4. **Start with easy, factual questions before anything sensitive.** Beginning with "walk me through your day" or "tell me about the last time you did X" warms someone up before asking about frustrations or failures.
5. **Ask about specific past behavior, not hypothetical future behavior.** "Tell me about the last time this happened" produces a more reliable answer than "would you use a feature that does X," which people are notoriously bad at predicting accurately about themselves.
6. **Follow up relentlessly with "why" and "tell me more."** The first answer someone gives is often surface-level — the most valuable insight usually comes two or three follow-up questions deeper.
7. **Resist the urge to pitch your product or defend a decision.** The moment you start selling or explaining, the person shifts from sharing their honest experience to reacting to you — which defeats the purpose of the interview.
8. **Take notes, or record with permission, and review soon after.** Memory fades fast, and patterns across several interviews are much easier to spot with accurate notes than from memory alone.

## Good questions vs bad questions

| Weak question | Why it's weak | Stronger alternative |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use a feature that does X?" | Asks someone to predict hypothetical future behavior, which is unreliable | "Tell me about the last time you needed to do something like X — what did you actually do?" |
| "Don't you think this would be easier with a dashboard?" | A leading question that suggests the answer | "Walk me through how you currently keep track of this." |
| "Do you like our product?" | Too general, invites a polite, unhelpful answer | "What's the most frustrating part of using this recently?" |
| "What features do you want?" | Asks for a solution, not the underlying problem | "What are you trying to accomplish when you run into that issue?" |

## Why past behavior beats hypothetical predictions

People are consistently unreliable at predicting their own future behavior, especially around hypothetical products or features they've never actually used. Someone might enthusiastically say they'd use a new feature in an interview, then never touch it once it's built — not out of dishonesty, but because imagining future behavior accurately is genuinely difficult. Asking about a specific, real, recent example instead ("tell me about the last time you tried to do this") anchors the conversation in what someone actually did, which is a far more reliable predictor of real behavior than a hypothetical guess.

## Common mistakes that ruin interview data

- **Asking leading questions that suggest the "right" answer.** Most people will politely agree with a leading question rather than push back, which produces false confirmation instead of real insight.
- **Talking too much.** A good interviewer should be listening for the large majority of the conversation — if you're talking more than the person you're interviewing, you're probably steering the conversation instead of learning from it.
- **Only interviewing people who already love the product.** This produces a skewed, overly positive picture — talking to people who churned, or who never adopted the product at all, often reveals more valuable, actionable insight.
- **Treating one interview as definitive.** A single conversation is one data point, not a pattern — real confidence comes from seeing the same theme repeat across multiple independent interviews.
- **Not writing down what you learned before it fades.** Insights from an interview are easy to misremember or blend together after just a few days without notes.

## FAQ

**How many user interviews are enough?**
There's no universal number, but many researchers find that 5-8 well-targeted interviews on a focused topic reveal most of the major recurring themes, with additional interviews mostly confirming rather than revealing new patterns — though this varies by how diverse your user base is and how focused your research question is.

**Should you interview users individually or in groups?**
Individually, in almost all cases. Group settings tend to produce socially influenced answers, where people agree with whoever spoke first or the most confidently, rather than sharing their own honest, independent perspective.

**Should you record user interviews?**
Yes, with the person's permission, since it lets you stay fully present in the conversation instead of splitting attention with note-taking, and it allows you to review exact wording later, which often matters more than a paraphrased summary.

**How is a user interview different from a customer support conversation?**
A support conversation is usually focused on resolving one specific issue for that person. A user interview is structured research, focused on understanding broader patterns and underlying needs, even if the specific person you're talking to isn't currently experiencing a problem.
